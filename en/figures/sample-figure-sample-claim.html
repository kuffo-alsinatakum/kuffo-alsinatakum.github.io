The Law of Fratricide and the Killing of Ahmed Çelebi

Applying the site's ten adopted standards and criteria to this specific claim.

The claim as stated: Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror is accused of killing his brother Ahmed Çelebi upon taking the throne in 855 AH / 1451 CE, said in some accounts to have been an infant or young child, and a so-called "Kanunname" (law of fratricide) was later attributed to him, permitting a sultan to kill his brothers to preserve the order of the state.

1. Source of the Claim and Its Historical Context

The accusation attached to Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror, may Allah have mercy on him, is that he killed his brother Ahmed Çelebi upon taking the throne in 855 AH / 1451 CE, said in some accounts to have been an infant or young child. A so-called "Kanunname" (law of fratricide) was later attributed to him, with the following text as it is reported:

"And to whichever of my sons the sultanate shall pass, it is fitting for him to kill his brothers in the interest of the order of the world [the state]. Most of the scholars have permitted this. Let action be taken accordingly." Original Ottoman text: «Ve her kimesneye evlâdımdan saltanat müyesser ola, karındaşların nizâm-ı âlem içün katl etmek münâsibdir. Ekser ulemâ dahi tecviz etmişlerdir. Anınla âmil olalar.»

This text and its discussion appear in the book The Unknown Ottoman State: Questions and Answers, by Prof. Dr. Ahmed Akgündüz and Prof. Dr. Said Öztürk.

2. Hypotheses and Juristic Grounding

It is not permissible, religiously or legally, to accuse a Muslim without proof, and this alone is sufficient grounds to reject the claim that the Conqueror killed his brother unless established by valid proof.

Even if we hypothetically assume this law is correctly attributed to the Conqueror, he did not invent it himself; rather, he relied on the rulings of scholars, based on the hadith of 'Arfajah reported by Bukhari in al-Tarikh al-Kabir and by Muslim, that the Prophet ﷺ said:

"There will be turbulence and division, so whoever seeks to divide the affairs of this unified community, strike him with the sword, whoever he may be."

This is an explicit and forceful text that whoever seeks discord and division of the Muslim community — even if one of the sultan's own sons — is judged by execution, since if such a person were not killed "whoever he may be," Muslims would end up killing one another and the state would collapse. Amputating a limb is sometimes what preserves the rest of the body, and this statement does not contradict the text of the hadith or the statements of the scholars. And Allah knows best.

3. Chain of Transmission Analysis

This narration has no chain of transmission at all. Consequently, the story has no basis whatsoever — not through an authentic chain, nor even a weak one — and it is not permissible to attribute it or use it as evidence.

As the authors themselves acknowledge, no original manuscript stating these laws has ever been found, neither from Ottoman historians nor from the opposing camp. Strikingly, the oldest copies currently circulating among researchers are found in states known for their hostility and wars against the Ottoman state (Austria, France, and Russia), and mostly date to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries CE — that is, a very long time after the Conqueror's death.

Even the well-known Ottoman legal scholar Himmet Berki denied the attribution of these forty-seven laws to Sultan Mehmed the Conqueror, claiming they were copies fabricated by Westerners hostile to the Ottoman state.

4. Applying the Ten Standards and Criteria

The claim below is measured against the ten levels adopted in the "Standards & Criteria" section, with the result recorded at each level.

1

Sahih al-Bukhari (the Transmission of Abu Dharr al-Harawi)

Result: Not applicable — Bukhari predates the Conqueror's era by centuries

2

Malik's Muwatta (the Transmission of al-Qa'nabi) / Greater and Lesser History

Result: Not applicable — Imam Malik predates the Conqueror's era by centuries

3

The Remaining Six Books and the Musnad

Result: Not applicable — the compilers of the Six Books and hadith collectors all predate the Conqueror's era

4

The Historians

Result: Not applicable — well-known historians such as al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir predate the Conqueror's era

5

The Testimony of Just Contemporaries

Result: Not a single contemporary known for justice, scholarship, and fairness testified that the Conqueror killed his brother or issued a law permitting such killing.

6

The Argument From Scholarly Silence

Result: This event or this law was not mentioned by a single scholar contemporary with the Conqueror anywhere in the Muslim world, figures such as Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti and Ibn Hajar al-Haytami — neither in condemnation, nor acknowledgment, nor even as a matter of discussion or a legal opinion.

7

The Absence of Any Invocation of the Narration by Opponents

Result: States whose relations with the Ottomans were tense, such as Hungary, Venice, the Papal State, and the Mamluks, never exploited this claim politically despite their clear interest in doing so had it been true. Worth noting: Imam al-Suyuti issued his rulings independently, was not one of the sultan's court scholars, and lived under Mamluk rule — and yet he mentioned nothing of this either.

8

Material and Archaeological Evidence

Result: No original manuscript stating these laws has ever been found; the oldest circulating copies date to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, meaning long after the Conqueror's death, and are found in states hostile to the Ottomans (detailed in the chain analysis section above).

9

Prophetic Exoneration of the Accused

Result: Assuming the authenticity of the hadith "What an excellent commander its commander will be" (which some scholars have authenticated and relied upon), it is evidence that contradicts the murder claim and further weakens it. So here we have a hadith whose authenticity is disputed, set against a claim that has no basis at all to begin with. (A complete study of this hadith's chain is provided in the second claim page: Verifying the Prophetic Bushra of the Conquest of Constantinople.)

10

Narrations From Known Enemies

Result: The source of the claim is Byzantine historians with established hostility toward the Ottomans: Doukas, and Nicolò Barbaro (who witnessed the fall of Constantinople and writes from an explicitly hostile perspective), and Laonikos Chalkokondyles.

5. Response to the Bukhari-vs-Kanunname Comparison Objection

The authors of The Unknown Ottoman State argued that the variations existing between copies of Sahih al-Bukhari — the most authentic book after the Book of Allah — cannot be grounds for doubting its authenticity, drawing a comparison to the Conqueror's Kanunname. This argument is rejected:

The authentic copies of Bukhari have no dispute among them: it is the transmission of Abu Dharr al-Harawi from his three teachers — al-Kushmihani, al-Sarakhsi, and al-Mustamli — from al-Farabri, the most precise and reliable of Bukhari's students, and this transmission currently exists in Turkey in both written and manuscript form, firmly established. So the chain is connected without any doubt.

The chain of the Conqueror's Kanunname, on the other hand, is not connected by hearing, nor in writing, nor even by acknowledgment from just contemporaries. So the comparison here is with a vast difference: a firmly established manuscript that certainly exists on one side, versus a complete absence of any original manuscript on the other.

6. Analysis of the Bishr al-Ghanawi Chain

This analysis relates to the hadith "What an excellent commander its commander will be," found in the prophetic exoneration of the conqueror of Constantinople (Standard 9 above), and it involves a problem in attributing the narration to the Companion "Bishr al-Ghanawi" (also said to be Abdullah or Ubaydullah ibn Bishr al-Ghanawi, an Egyptian mentioned by Ibn Hibban in the generation of the Companions, of whom Abu Hatim said: "Egyptian, had companionship [with the Prophet]").

The first problem: the narration comes through a Kufan route, from an Egyptian, from a Kufan, and this alone is not sufficient to establish the rank of authentication, because Ibn Hibban is known for being lenient in authenticating narrators.

The second problem: Ibn al-Salah, al-Nawawi, and Hafiz Zayn al-Din al-'Iraqi (may Allah have mercy on them) established in their introductions to hadith sciences that a narrator being recognized as a Companion is known by one of four means: mass transmission (tawatur), widespread but non-mass-level fame, the narration of another established Companion about him, or his own statement about himself being a Companion after his integrity has been independently established.

Bishr al-Ghanawi's companionship was not established by any of the first three means, leaving only the fourth means; and since there is no independent evidence establishing his integrity, it is not valid to rely on his own statement about himself that he was a Companion or that he heard [directly] from the Prophet ﷺ.

Conclusion of this analysis: This hadith is not established through this route, nor was anything similar transmitted among the Companions or those who came after them who attempted to conquer Constantinople in pursuit of this glad tiding.

Conclusion and Final Verdict

The conclusion regarding this matter: an accusation of murder is not established by mere claim; it requires evidence, proof, or the testimony of just witnesses. In this case, there is no valid evidence, no proof, and no just witnesses, nor is any condemnation transmitted from the scholars of his era — and it was not even established that his enemies accused him of killing his brothers, even though such an accusation would have been a valuable opportunity for them to tarnish his reputation and attack him, had it had any basis.

As for what is known as the "law of fratricide," no authentic manuscript exists establishing its text; indeed, the original document it is attributed to does not exist. Nor is it known that the scholars contemporary with him, across the various regions, ever condemned or objected to it, nor is it transmitted from a single God-fearing scholar that he openly condemned it or considered it an injustice or oppression.

Finally, even if we hypothetically concede that he did say this statement or approve this law, the religious ruling regarding one who threatens the unity of the Muslim community and seeks to divide it is well established in Islamic jurisprudence, and has been affirmed by the majority of scholars. The scholars expressed this meaning by saying: amputating a limb is preferable to losing the whole body.
