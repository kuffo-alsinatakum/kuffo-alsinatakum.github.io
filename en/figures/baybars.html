Al-Zahir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari, may Allah have mercy on him

(620 – 676 AH / 1223 – 1277 CE)

One of the sultans of the Mamluk state, the vanquisher of the Mongols and the Crusaders, and the reviver of the Abbasid caliphate in Cairo. The historical narrations have leveled accusations at him that were transmitted with no connected chain and no legally valid evidence.

He is Sultan al-Malik al-Zahir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari, one of the sultans of the Mamluk state. He came originally from the Qipchaq tribes north of the Black Sea, was taken captive as a boy and sold as a mamluk, and was ascribed to Amir Ala al-Din Aydakin al-Bunduqdari, in whose service he was raised. He rose by military competence until he became one of the senior commanders of the Bahri mamluks, and then assumed the sultanate in 658 AH.

His most well-established achievements:

  • His conduct at the Battle of al-Mansura in 648 AH against the Seventh Crusade led by Louis IX, king of France, where he was among the foremost commanders of a battle that ended with the defeat of the campaign and the capture of its king.
  • His command of the vanguard of the army at the Battle of Ain Jalut in 658 AH, which broke the legend that the Mongols could not be defeated and halted their advance toward Egypt and the Hijaz.
  • His sustained resistance to Mongol raids after Ain Jalut, and the securing of the frontiers of Syria and the Jazira, most notably his victory at the Battle of Elbistan in 675 AH.
  • The revival of the Abbasid caliphate in Cairo in 659 AH after the fall of Baghdad, when he gave the pledge of allegiance to the caliph al-Mustansir Billah, restoring to the community its unifying symbol after its interruption.
  • His successive conquests of the Crusader principalities in Syria: Caesarea, Arsuf, Safad, Jaffa, and Shaqif, then Antioch in 666 AH and Hisn al-Akrad (Krak des Chevaliers) in 669 AH, decisively shrinking the Crusader presence.
  • Subduing the fortresses of the Isma'ilis (the Assassins) in the mountains of Syria and ending the danger they posed.
  • His appointment of four judges from the four schools of law in 663 AH, a judicial arrangement that remained in force for centuries after him.
  • The establishment of a regular postal system between Cairo and Damascus, the renewal of the fleet, the restoration of citadels, fortresses, and bridges, and the digging of canals.
  • His religious and scholarly construction: the Zahiri Mosque in Cairo, and the Zahiriyya school in Cairo and in Damascus.
  • His care for the two Holy Sanctuaries and the pilgrimage route: securing the route, providing the covering of the Kaaba, and carrying out repairs in the two Holy Sanctuaries.

He passed away, may Allah have mercy on him, in Damascus in 676 AH and was buried there, leaving behind a stable and powerful state that had faced the Mongols and the Crusaders at one and the same time, and an administrative and military structure that remained the mainstay of the Mamluk state after him.
