The Accusation of Killing Sultan al-Mu'azzam Turan Shah

Applying the eleven standards and criteria adopted on this site to this particular claim.

The claim as stated: Amir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari is accused of being the one who killed Sultan al-Malik al-Mu'azzam Ghiyath al-Din Turan Shah, son of al-Malik al-Salih Ayyub and the last of the Ayyubid sultans in Egypt, in the camp at Fariskur in 648 AH, after his victory over the Seventh Crusade and the capture of Louis IX. Some transmissions state that he was the one who began the attack with the first blow, cutting off the sultan's fingers.

1. Defining the Question at Issue

The incident under study is the killing of Sultan al-Malik al-Mu'azzam Ghiyath al-Din Turan Shah, son of al-Malik al-Salih Najm al-Din Ayyub, in the army camp at Fariskur in 648 AH, some seventy days after he had come from Hisn Kayfa to take the kingship, and after the victory over the Seventh Crusade and the capture of Louis IX, king of France. With his killing the Ayyubid state in Egypt came to an end and the Mamluk state arose.

The question at issue here is not the occurrence of the killing itself, for that was a public event witnessed by many and followed by enormous political consequences. The question at issue is rather the attribution of the killing to Amir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari in particular, and the claim that he carried it out with his own hand and began it with the first blow, which is the detail upon which the charge is built.

2. Surveying and Critiquing the Sources for the Incident

The incident is mentioned in two layers of sources: contemporaries of the event, and those later than it. All of them without exception relate the report as unattributed narrative, not with a connected chain of transmission. What follows are the texts of both layers:

Sibt ibn al-Jawzi (d. 654 AH, a contemporary) in Mir'at al-Zaman

His text: "On Monday the twenty-seventh of Muharram al-Mu'azzam sat at the table, and one of the Bahri mamluks struck him with a sword. He met it with his hand, and some of his fingers were cut off. He rose and entered the tower and cried out: 'Who wounded me?' They said: 'The Isma'ili assassins.' He said: 'No, by Allah, it was none but the Bahriyya. By Allah, I will leave none of them remaining.'" And at the end of his report: "Those who carried out his killing were four."

This is the earliest of the contemporaries, and he names no one in particular, neither for the first blow nor for the carrying out of the killing. He reports that the perpetrators were four, without naming a single one of them.

Abu Shama (d. 665 AH, a contemporary) in al-Dhayl ala al-Rawdatayn

He mentions that the news arrived in Safar of 648 AH that al-Mu'azzam had been killed, that he was struck with a sword and fled, that he entered the wooden tower and it was set on fire, that he threw himself toward the Nile, and that he was overtaken and cut down at the village of Fariskur, "and that was at the hands of his father's Bahri retainers."

The wording is therefore in the plural with no naming, and it states plainly that Abu Shama was in Damascus and that the news reached him. He is thus a transmitter of an incoming report, not a witness of the incident.

Ibn Wasil (d. 697 AH, a contemporary who resided in Egypt) in Mufarrij al-Kurub

He alone names Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari as entering upon al-Mu'azzam and striking him, states that the blow fell between two of his fingers and wounded him only slightly, that he then threw the sword from his hand and fled, and that the killing occurred only afterward, when the Bahriyya and their commander Faris al-Din Aqtay came upon al-Mu'azzam in the wooden tower.

Ibn Kathir (d. 774 AH) in al-Bidaya wa al-Nihaya

He contradicts him explicitly in the identification, saying: "One of the amirs struck him, namely Izz al-Din Aybak al-Turkmani, and he struck him on his hand and cut off some of his fingers." He then mentions Baybars as a later striker rather than the first, saying: "Among those who struck him was al-Bunduqdari, on his shoulder, so that the sword came out from beneath his other armpit."

Ibn Taghribirdi (d. 874 AH) in al-Nujum al-Zahira

Later than the event by more than two centuries. He describes the incident as the sultan sitting in audience with the amirs before him, when a group of the Bahri mamluks advanced upon him with swords in their hands and struck his fingers and cut them off, whereupon he rose and fled and entered a wooden tower, which was then set ablaze, so he threw himself into the water and died burned, slain, and drowned. The wording is in the plural, and it names no particular person as the one who delivered the first blow, and he says: "Those who carried out his killing were four."

Ibn Iyas (d. 930 AH) in Bada'i al-Zuhur

Later than the event by about three centuries. He transmits details about Turan Shah's temperament and about his saying that he would do to the Bahri mamluks what he was doing to the candles. These are psychological and conversational details that cannot be established by any chain. The origin of this account is mentioned before him in al-Maqrizi's al-Suluk and in Ibn Taghribirdi, so it is not unique to him.

The Account of the Crusader Captives

The closest thing to an eyewitness account of this incident is what was transmitted by the captives of the Crusade who were being held in the camp at the time of the event, foremost among them Jean de Joinville. These were warring enemies who had just fallen into captivity, and their account falls away by the express terms of the tenth standard adopted on this site.

The Instability of the Sources in the Most Specific Details of the Report

First: instability over who delivered the first blow. This is the very detail upon which the charge is built, and there are three mutually exclusive positions on it: no one in particular, in Sibt ibn al-Jawzi and Abu Shama, the earliest of the contemporaries, and in Ibn Taghribirdi with the plural form; Baybars al-Bunduqdari, in Ibn Wasil; and Izz al-Din Aybak al-Turkmani, in Ibn Kathir, who makes Baybars a later striker on the shoulder rather than the author of the first blow.

Second: instability over the number. "Four" in Sibt ibn al-Jawzi and Ibn Taghribirdi, without naming them, and "a group" in others.

Third: instability over the date. In the transmission from Sibt ibn al-Jawzi it was Monday the twenty-seventh of Muharram, while in the transmission from the critical edition of Mir'at al-Zaman it was the twenty-eighth of it.

Fourth: the statement of the victim himself. He was asked, "Who wounded you?" and he was the one who saw the man who struck him, and he said: "No, by Allah, it was none but the Bahriyya," without specifying anyone by name.

Methodological note: the texts of Sibt ibn al-Jawzi and Ibn Wasil recorded here have reached us through intermediate transmissions from the critical editions, and it remains necessary to collate them against the printed originals: Mufarrij al-Kurub (6/128 onward), Mir'at al-Zaman (22/413 onward), and al-Dhayl ala al-Rawdatayn. This collation does not change the outcome of this study, because what the study turns on is the divergence of the sources over the identification, and that is established through more than one route.

3. Chain of Transmission Analysis

This incident has no chain of transmission at all. The Ayyubid and Mamluk history books do not relate their reports with chains, but rather in the form of unattributed narrative: "a group of the mamluks advanced upon him," and "Shajar al-Durr agreed with so-and-so and so-and-so." Nowhere in any of them is there "so-and-so related to me, saying: I witnessed," nor anything that stands in its place. There is no direct hearing, no act of transmission, and no route that could be measured against the rules of impugnment and validation (al-jarh wa al-ta'dil).

Nor is that changed by the appearance of a particular name in some of these books, because the naming occurs within that same unattributed narrative, with no hearing, no act of transmission, and no route. It is therefore a report without a chain like any other, neither testimony nor transmission.

Since there is no chain, there is no place for the study of the narrators, for one cannot examine the probity or the precision of a transmitter in a report that has no transmitter to begin with. We therefore move directly to measuring the claim against the eleven standards and criteria.

4. Measured Against the Eleven Levels

What follows is the claim measured against the eleven levels adopted on this site, in their own order:

1

The Time Condition

Result: A source counts only if it was written after the event. The killing of Turanshah in 648 AH postdates al-Bukhari, the Muwatta, and all the collections of the Sunnah, so the event could not be expected to appear in them; what remains for examination are the historians who came after it.

2

Sahih al-Bukhari (the Transmission of Abu Dharr al-Harawi)

Result: Not applicable, since al-Bukhari precedes the time of the incident by centuries.

3

The Muwatta of Imam Malik (the Transmission of al-Qa'nabi), and al-Bukhari's al-Tarikh al-Kabir and al-Tarikh al-Awsat

Result: Not applicable, since Imam Malik precedes the time of the incident by centuries.

4

The Remaining Six Books and the Musnad

Result: Not applicable, since the authors of the Six Books precede the time of the incident by centuries.

5

The Historians

Result: This is the only level that applies. The sources here are numerous and close to one another in time, yet all of them are without a connected chain, and the earliest of them in wording mentions "a group of the Bahri mamluks" in the plural, not any particular person.

6

The Testimony of Just Contemporaries Not Suspected of Bias

Result: Not met. No two just Muslim witnesses have been established who saw the incident with their own eyes and affirmed that Baybars was the one who carried out the killing. The contemporary historians were not present, and they did not transmit from anyone who was present by way of a chain.

7

The Argument From Scholarly Silence

Result: No explicit, documented condemnation of Baybars by name over this incident has been established from any of the recognized scholars of the age.

It is inconceivable that the scholars of the age would agree upon silence over an established killing whose perpetrator was known, given that they were people who enjoined right and forbade wrong.

The clearest illustration of this is the case of Sibt ibn al-Jawzi (d. 654 AH), the most famous of the preachers of Syria and the boldest of them toward kings, with his recorded precedent of openly condemning al-Malik al-Kamil when he handed over Jerusalem. He recounted the killing of Turan Shah in a manner dripping with disapproval, mentioning his plea for help: "I do not want the kingship. Let me return to the fortress. O Muslims, is there none among you who will take me under his protection?" and that the armies were all standing by and none of them answered him, and that he remained on the riverbank for three days with no one daring to bury him until the caliph's envoy interceded for him. Then he named no perpetrator in particular, but said instead: "one of the Bahri mamluks."

Nor is it sound to excuse that by fear, for he was in Damascus under the rule of al-Nasir Yusuf the Ayyubid, who was an opponent of the Mamluks in Egypt and later fought them. Naming and denouncing would therefore have agreed with the inclination of the ruler of the land, not gone against it. The motives for naming were all present and the impediments were absent, and still he did not specify. The silence of such a man over the identification, while he was able to make it and safe in doing so, is evidence that the identification was not established in his view.

8

The Absence of Any Invocation of the Narration by Opponents

Result: Met, for the closest of the opponents, and the one with the greatest interest in naming a culprit, moved on the basis of a collective charge rather than an individual one, and then accepted the very man accused into his service, granted him an iqta, and relied upon him for years.

The import of this standard is that if the accusation had been current and known among contemporaries, the first people to publicize it would be the opponents of the accused, above all one who had kinship with the victim and a claim to avenge his blood, and who had a direct political interest in raising it.

All of this was present in al-Malik al-Nasir Yusuf, the ruler of Aleppo. He was the victim's paternal cousin and of the house of Ayyub, and within weeks of the incident he moved and took Damascus, then marched with his army to Egypt to fight the Mamluks. If the killing had been attributed to one particular man, this was the moment for that naming to be raised and for the people to be roused by it.

Ibn Kathir said in al-Bidaya wa al-Nihaya, under the events of the year 648 AH: "Then the month of Muharram had not ended before the amirs killed the son of their master, Turan Shah, and buried him beside the Nile on the other side." And he said, in describing that very movement of the people of Aleppo: "When there occurred in the lands of Egypt the killing of al-Mu'azzam Turan Shah, son of al-Salih Ayyub, by the amirs, the people of Aleppo rode out, and with them the son of their master, al-Nasir Yusuf, son of al-Aziz Muhammad, son of al-Zahir Ghazi... and they came to Damascus and besieged it and took it swiftly."

The declared motive of the campaign is thus attributed to "the amirs" in the plural, twice over: once in describing the incident, and once in describing the campaign built upon it. In neither place is any particular perpetrator named.

A campaign raised to avenge blood is not raised against an unknown party. Had the perpetrator been specified and known, mentioning his name would have been more effective in the call and more unifying for the people than invoking an unnamed group. So the vagueness about the perpetrator on the tongue of the opponent himself is an indication that the identification was not established at that time.

The matter here goes beyond mere silence about the naming to something still more telling. Four years after the incident, when the commander Aqtay was killed in Egypt in 652 AH and the Bahri mamluks left Cairo for Syria, their destination was al-Malik al-Nasir Yusuf himself.

Al-Maqrizi said in al-Mawa'iz wa al-I'tibar bi Dhikr al-Khitat wa al-Athar (2/245): "They agreed together to leave Egypt for Syria, and the greatest of them at that time was Baybars al-Bunduqdari, along with Qalawun al-Alfi, Sunqur al-Ashqar, Baysari, Sukuz, and Baramiq." Then he said: "As for those men, they went to al-Malik al-Nasir Yusuf, son of al-Aziz, the ruler of Syria, and he received them, bestowed favors upon them, granted them iqtas, and relied upon them." He then mentions that they remained in Syria until Qutuz became sultan, whereupon they returned to Egypt in his days.

Ibn Kathir names Baybars by name in al-Nasir's army down to his final days, saying under the events of the year 658 AH, concerning al-Nasir and al-Mughith: "He was at war along with al-Nasir, the ruler of Damascus, against the Egyptians, and with them was Amir Rukn al-Din Baybars al-Bunduqdari, and they had resolved to fight the Egyptians and take Egypt from them." And he said, describing the collapse of al-Nasir's position before the Tatars: "When al-Nasir learned of that he fled to the citadel and the armies scattered in all directions, and Amir Rukn al-Din Baybars rode off with his companions toward Gaza."

During that period an estrangement occurred between al-Nasir and the Bahriyya, so they parted from him to Karak and then returned to him. The acceptance was therefore not once but twice.

What these texts establish is that al-Malik al-Nasir Yusuf, the victim's paternal cousin and the author of the declared call to avenge his blood, received these men, bestowed favors upon them, granted them iqtas, and relied upon them in his army, Baybars foremost among them, and that Baybars remained with him until the year 658 AH. This is not merely silence about the naming; it is the practical conduct of the man with the greatest claim to demand the blood, and it cannot be reconciled with a specific, established accusation against Baybars in particular in his view.

Methodological control: al-Maqrizi and Ibn Kathir are later transmitters, not contemporaries, and the absence of a naming is an absence in the narration, not a denial of the fact in itself. We have not obtained the text of al-Nasir Yusuf's correspondence and propaganda, which is found in Ibn Wasil's Mufarrij al-Kurub and Abu Shama's al-Dhayl ala al-Rawdatayn, and access to these two was not possible. This standard is therefore presented as one indication joined to its fellows, not as an independent and decisive proof.

9

Material and Archaeological Evidence

Result: There is no material evidence specifying who delivered the blow, and no contemporary investigative document. All that is available are narrative texts.

10

Quranic or Prophetic Commendation of the Accused

Result: Not directly applicable, since there is no specific prophetic commendation known of Baybars in particular that could be set against this narration.

11

Narrations From Known Enemies

Result: Strongly applicable in this case. The closest thing that can be described as an eyewitness account is the report of the captives of the Crusade who were being held in the camp, and they were warring enemies who had just been taken captive, so their account falls away by the express terms of this standard.

5. Hypotheses and Their Discussion

The First Hypothesis: that Baybars killed Turan Shah with his own hand and began it with the first blow

This hypothesis requires at least two just witnesses according to the condition of legal proof, and that is not met. Furthermore, the sources themselves differ over the identification of the perpetrator: the earliest of the contemporaries names no one and says "one of the Bahri mamluks," Ibn Wasil names Baybars, and Ibn Kathir names Aybak al-Turkmani and makes Baybars a later striker on the shoulder. When a report is unstable in its most specific details, no charge can be built upon it.

The Second Hypothesis: that the killing was carried out by a group of the Mamluk amirs

With no decisive determination of who in particular delivered the fatal blow. This hypothesis is closer to what the wording of the sources themselves can bear.

The Third Hypothesis: that the specification of names and details belongs to later narrative additions

That is, that the naming of Baybars, Qalawun, and Aqtay, the details of the dialogue, and the image of the sword and the candles are among the additions that formed after the rise of the Mamluk state, in order to explain the transfer of rule to them. Two things strengthen this hypothesis: that the earliest contemporaries are the sources most free of any naming, and that the victim himself, when asked about the man who struck him, specified no one but said: "No, by Allah, it was none but the Bahriyya."

Conclusion and Final Verdict

Here a distinction must be drawn between two matters that may not be confused with one another:

The first: the public, witnessed event. This is the killing of Turan Shah in the army camp in 648 AH, the fall of the Ayyubid state in Egypt, and the rise of the Mamluk state. An entire army witnessed this, and political consequences followed from it that changed the face of the region, so it is established by widespread transmission and by its witnessed effects, not by a chain.

The second: the particular detail upon which the charge is built. That is, who delivered the blow with his own hand, who intended what, and what was said in the tent. This can be established only by a connected chain and the testimony of just witnesses, and that is entirely absent in this incident. There is no chain, no just and unbiased witness, and no material evidence, and the only witness who was present was a Crusader enemy whose account falls away by our tenth standard.

Accordingly, the attribution of the killing of Turan Shah to al-Zahir Baybars singly and with a documented chain has not been established by the standard required in this methodology. On the contrary, the sources that have been passed along without any chain differ over who delivered the first blow along three distinct positions, and what they do agree upon is that Turan Shah was killed at the hands of a group of the Bahri mamluks.

For a report to be accepted, and above all for a Muslim or any human being to be convicted of a crime such as killing, it must satisfy the legal, customary, and juridical conditions, and that is not available in this matter.

"O you who have believed, if there comes to you a disobedient one with information, investigate, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become, over what you have done, regretful." (Surah Al-Hujurat 49:6). And in another reading: "then ascertain the truth."
